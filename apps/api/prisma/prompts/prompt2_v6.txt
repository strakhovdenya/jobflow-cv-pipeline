=== EVIDENCE SOURCE RULES ===

Use only the vacancy text, the Prompt 1 analysis and the knowledge-source references provided in the input context. When a selected knowledge source's content is inlined in the input context, treat that content as real evidence and use it directly for bullet wording and claims. Never invent commercial experience, employers, clients, metrics or technologies not explicitly present in the provided context — this includes not going beyond what an inlined source actually states. Never invent numeric metrics or percentages that are not explicitly stated in the provided context.

**Canonical terms must match evidence verbatim.** When you reuse a proper technical name from the evidence — a technology, library, framework, protocol, product, platform, data format, standard or file format — write it exactly as the evidence writes it. A semantically-close substitute is a factual error here, not a stylistic variation: a near-synonym, a sibling technology from the same family, an expanded or contracted form, a more familiar-sounding alternative, or a more generic category name in place of the specific one all change what the CV claims the candidate has worked with, and a reader or interviewer will read it as the thing you named, not the thing in the evidence. If you are not certain the evidence uses a given name, either use the exact string the evidence does use, or describe the work without naming that specific term at all. Never "improve", modernize or normalize a technical name toward one that seems more standard or more marketable. This applies everywhere a name can appear: `headline`, `summary[]`, `top_skills[]`, every `bullets[].text`, and every `tech_stack[]`.

**What this rule does not govern: how you name a kind of work.** The verbatim rule is about proper names — something that was shipped under that name and can be looked up: `BullMQ`, `CommerceTools`, `FastAPI`, `Azure Durable Functions`, `PostgreSQL`. It says nothing about the ordinary descriptive phrase for a category of work: "API integrations", "AI implementation", "data pipelines", "background job processing", "production monitoring". Those are not technical names, have no canonical spelling to violate, and cannot be got wrong the way a product name can. So when the vacancy has its own phrase for a kind of work the candidate genuinely did, use the vacancy's phrase. Do not retreat to the underlying protocol, or to the broader category, because that feels closer to the evidence's literal words: writing "REST APIs" where the vacancy asked for "API integrations", or "personal AI projects" where it asked for "AI implementation", is not a safer version of the same statement — it answers a question the vacancy did not ask and leaves the reader to do the mapping. The verbatim rule and the vacancy's vocabulary only ever collide when you are about to rename an actual technology, and there the verbatim rule wins, unchanged.

=== INTERNAL REASONING NEVER BECOMES PUBLIC CV TEXT ===

You produce two kinds of content in this output, and they must never mix.

**Public fields** — everything inside `cv_content` — are read by a recruiter or hiring manager. They contain only what the candidate did, framed positively and honestly.

**Internal fields** — `requirement_coverage`, `evidence_table`, `overclaiming_check`, `target_strategy`, `pdf_readiness_notes` — are read by the pipeline and by the candidate. This is where your audit reasoning belongs: which requirements you could not cover, which claims lack evidence, which risks you weighed, what you decided not to show and why.

A sentence whose function is to state what is missing, unproven, weak or absent from the candidate's background must never appear inside any `cv_content` field. Concretely, do not write into public text: statements that a requirement is not demonstrated in this background; statements that a technology was used only in a personal or learning context *phrased as a disclaimer about the candidate* rather than as a factual description of the project; parenthetical caveats appended to a claim to pre-empt doubt about it; or any sentence a reader would recognize as the CV auditing itself. If you found a gap, that finding goes in `requirement_coverage.reason_if_not_shown` and/or `overclaiming_check.needs_evidence` — and then the public CV simply does not make the claim, silently.

The distinction is function, not vocabulary. "Built a FastAPI service as a personal project" is a factual description of what the work was, and is fine. "This is personal project work and not commercial production experience" is the audit talking about the candidate's limitations, and is not. Honest framing of what something *is* stays; commentary about what the candidate *lacks* goes to the internal fields.

=== REQUIREMENT COVERAGE — DO THIS BEFORE WRITING ANY BULLET ===

`requirement_coverage` is an internal planning artifact. It is never rendered into the CV; the renderer ignores it entirely. Its purpose is to make your ranking decisions explicit and checkable instead of an invisible side effect of free-text generation.

Build it as follows, before you write `headline`, `summary`, or any bullet:

1. Take every entry in the `=== PROMPT 1 ANALYSIS ===` block's `must_have` list, every entry in its `nice_to_have` list, and any requirement implied by `hidden_role_logic`. These are the requirements. Do not invent requirements the vacancy did not express, and do not silently drop one because the candidate looks weak on it.
2. **Sweep the knowledge sources for every technology the vacancy names.** List every proper name the vacancy mentions — technology, library, framework, tool, platform, method, protocol, certification subject. For each one, search the inlined `=== KNOWLEDGE SOURCES ===` content for that exact name before you decide anything about it. Do not answer this from the project descriptions written into this prompt: those are abbreviated framing summaries, and they omit much of what the candidate has actually done. The inlined sources are the real inventory. A technology this prompt's own project lists never mention may still be present in the sources — surfacing that case is what this step is for. Record what the sweep found; a requirement you are about to mark `"transferable"` or `"not_shown"` must survive this search first. The sweep's job is to locate candidates for evidence, not to produce them: it is equally successful when it confirms that nothing supports a requirement, and the step immediately below decides which of the two a given hit actually is.

   **A name appearing in a source is not the same as evidence for it — read what the source says about it.** Knowledge sources describe work the candidate did *and* draw explicit boundaries around what must not be claimed, and both mention the technology by name. A hit inside a "do not claim", "what not to quantify", "not yet implemented", "future design target", "needs evidence" or "open question" passage is the source telling you the opposite of evidence: it is a prohibition, and it makes that requirement `"none"`/`"not_shown"`, never `"strong"`. Before counting any sweep hit, read the sentence it sits in and the heading above it, and ask which of the two kinds of statement you are looking at. Treat a bare name match — a list entry, a table cell, a caveat — as unresolved until you have read its context; only a passage that actually describes the candidate doing work with that technology is evidence. The same applies to a quantified result: a figure named in a "what not to quantify" list or flagged as an unresolved measurement is not available to the CV, however much the vacancy would reward it.

   **A reference or previously-produced CV is never a source of truth.** If the input context contains an earlier CV, an example CV or any comparison document that claims something the knowledge sources prohibit, the sources win without exception. Another document making a claim is not evidence that the claim is supportable — it is exactly the kind of unverified assertion these rules exist to keep out, and matching it would import its error.
3. For each requirement, emit one entry:
   - `requirement` — the requirement, in the vacancy's own terms.
   - `priority` — `"must_have"`, `"nice_to_have"`, or `"hidden"` (from `hidden_role_logic`).
   - `evidence_selected` — the specific piece of the candidate's evidence you chose to demonstrate it (a career case, an employer's work, a project facet), or `"none"` if nothing supports it.
   - `shown_in` — where in the CV that evidence actually appears, as a field path (e.g. `"experience[0].bullets[2]"`, `"current_work_block.bullets[1]"`, `"summary[0]"`, `"top_skills"`). Use `"not_shown"` when the requirement is not demonstrated anywhere in the CV.
   - `strength` — `"strong"` (directly evidenced commercial work), `"partial"` (evidenced but narrower or shorter than the requirement), `"transferable"` (adjacent evidence a reader would accept as related), or `"none"`.
   - `reason_if_not_shown` — required whenever `shown_in` is `"not_shown"`; `null` otherwise. State plainly why: no evidence exists, or it lost a space trade-off against a specific higher-priority requirement (name it).
4. Then write the CV so that every `shown_in` you claimed is true. A field path in `requirement_coverage` that points at a bullet you did not actually write is a broken output, worse than an honest `"not_shown"`.

**When the vacancy accepts non-commercial evidence, it counts at full strength.** Some vacancies say outright that they will take personal-project, portfolio, study or self-directed experience for a requirement — phrasings like "even from personal projects", "exposure through personal use", "familiarity", "an interest in", or an entry-level framing that asks to have seen something rather than to have shipped it commercially. When the vacancy sets that bar for a requirement, personal evidence meets it: judge `strength` against what the vacancy actually asked for, not against a commercial-production standard it never set, and present that work with the same confidence as any other bullet. Hedging it, or downgrading it to `"transferable"` because it is not commercial, understates a requirement the candidate genuinely satisfies. The framing rules do not change — personal work is still described as personal and never implied to be commercial, and `top_skills` still admits only confirmed commercial/core skills — but within those bounds the work is stated plainly, not apologetically. Where the vacancy does demand commercial depth for a requirement, the usual standard applies unchanged. And this rule only ever adjusts the bar that *existing* evidence is judged against — it never creates evidence. A requirement the sweep resolved as prohibited or unimplemented stays `"none"`/`"not_shown"` no matter how low a bar the vacancy sets for it: a vacancy asking only for "familiarity" with something the sources say was never built does not make it claimable, it just means the gap costs less.

**The ranking rule this exists to enforce:** a `must_have` requirement backed by confirmed evidence must not end up `"not_shown"`. The only acceptable reason for one to go unshown is that the space it needed went to another *requirement* — never that it lost the slot to a bullet covering nothing the vacancy asked for. Before finalizing, re-read the CV against this map: if a `must_have` is `"not_shown"` while some bullet in the CV covers no requirement at all, replace that bullet. Content the vacancy did not ask for is what gets cut first, always.

This is also what makes the CV vacancy-specific. Two vacancies producing the same `requirement_coverage` shape and the same bullets means the ranking step did not actually run.

Three rules govern these lists, and the first one gates the other two:

- **Every name in an entry's `tech_stack` must be traceable to that entry's own evidence.** This is a hard precondition, checked per entry and per name, before either rule below applies at all. The question is never "has the candidate used this somewhere" or "does this suit the vacancy" — it is "does the evidence for THIS employer, THIS period, THIS project show this technology". A name that drifted in from another entry's stack, from the candidate's general profile, or from the vacancy's wish list is a factual error: it tells a reader the candidate used that technology in work where they did not, and it fails at the first interview question about that role. Breadth is never a reason to include a name. When a specific entry's evidence does not place a technology in that work, leave it out of that entry and keep it where its evidence actually is. Calibrate the bar correctly, though: the evidence mentioning the technology in connection with that role, project or period is sufficient — do not demand more proof than the evidence format offers, and do not drop a name the entry's evidence does place there because the entry's bullets happen not to mention it. Turning it up in the technology sweep and then omitting it from the one field a reader scans for it wastes the sweep entirely.

Trimming those to make a list look focused makes the entry look narrower than the candidate actually is — this was tried and measurably lowered the result, so it is not to be reintroduced. This rule protects only names that already passed the first gate; it is never a reason to keep, or to reach for, one that did not.

Bullet slots are a fixed budget and must be rationed by requirement relevance; an evidence-backed `tech_stack` is not, and must not be rationed as if it were.

Ordering costs nothing, removes nothing, and is not a back door to the trimming this rule forbids. Rules for building the bullet from this menu:
- Lead with the facets that cover this vacancy's `must_have` requirements. If the vacancy is about data modelling and workflow correctness, the data layer and state-machine facets lead; if it is about API design and testing discipline, those do. The facet you lead with must be the facet the vacancy asked for — and it must be one this project's evidence actually supports, never one that merely shares a topic with the requirement.
- Keep it to one whole bullet — JobFlow's purpose and its selected features stay combined, never split into several bullets. It does not get one. Before finalizing the block, count how many of its bullets describe JobFlow: the answer must be exactly one. If two do, you have not added coverage — you have spent a second slot on a project the reader has already met, while the bullet count stayed the same, so something else lost its place. - Select 2-4 facets. Listing all of them turns the bullet into a feature dump that says nothing about fit; listing one usually wastes the project's range.

- **Name the technology, not only the capability.** Where a facet above names a concrete technology, carry that name into the bullet text and into `current_work_block.tech_stack` — a vacancy asking for a specific ORM, framework or API-documentation format is matched by that name appearing, not by a capability described in general words that happens to mean the same thing. A reader scanning for the name they asked for does not perform that translation, and neither does an automated scan. Describing the facet abstractly while its name sits in the evidence is how an evidence-backed requirement silently becomes an apparent gap. The converse never applies: this rule surfaces a name the facet above actually carries, and never supplies one a facet withholds — where a facet states that a technology is not implemented, that is the evidence, and no requirement makes it nameable.

=== PROFESSIONAL EXPERIENCE ===

Order experience entries most-recent-first. The candidate's employment history (use these exact companies, roles and dates unless the input context explicitly overrides them):

1. **EPAM Systems** — Backend-focused Fullstack Developer — Nov 2021 – May 2025 — `experience_type: "commercial"`. The primary record of commercial production work; must visually and substantively dominate over the current-work block and any personal projects. Give 6-8 bullets. Each bullet must connect to a vacancy requirement and cite an `evidence_source` (e.g. Career_Case_Deep_Dives). For early-stage startup/product-engineer/founder-facing/customer-facing vacancies, prioritize impact-first bullets (business value, user/product-facing workflow, reliability, scale, automation, production debugging) confirmed by the provided context — favor concrete, already-evidenced impact cases such as automation that cut a manual process from hours to minutes, reliability/scale improvements on a high-traffic integration, product-data-handling correctness at scale, or production incident/debugging work, when the provided context actually supports them for this candidate. Do not claim direct customer/product ownership without evidence — mark it `needs evidence` instead.
2. **Factor–IT** — PHP Backend Developer — Dec 2016 – Jun 2021 — `experience_type: "commercial"`. Give 3-5 bullets. Position this as backend foundation, never as a current PHP target stack: PostgreSQL, migrations, data integrity, complex business logic, production financial systems.
3. **CHI Software** — Node.js Developer Intern / Trainee — Jul 2021 – Oct 2021 — `experience_type: "commercial"`. Keep to 1-2 short bullets, framed only as the transition point into Node.js.
4. Earlier PHP role (if present in the provided context): include only if there is space and it does not pull positioning back toward PHP; otherwise omit it entirely rather than including a weak entry.

**One case, one bullet.** Within a single experience entry, each career case or piece of work gets exactly one bullet. Do not describe the same case twice from two angles — its technical side in one bullet and its business impact in another, or its scale in one and its implementation in another. Choose the single angle this vacancy's requirements make most relevant and write that one bullet; the second angle is a spent slot that a different, uncovered requirement should have had. Combining both angles into one bullet is fine when both genuinely serve the same requirement.

**A confirmed outcome outranks internal tooling for the same slot.** When two candidate bullets compete for one slot in an entry, and one carries a result the evidence confirms — a measured improvement, a scale figure, a data volume, a before/after — while the other describes internal, in-house or legacy tooling the vacancy did not ask about, the bullet with the result takes the slot. A reader can weigh an outcome; they cannot weigh an in-house framework they have never seen and cannot compare to anything, and it demonstrates nothing the vacancy asked for. Three limits on this. It is never permission to invent, round or extrapolate a figure — only a result already stated in the evidence counts, and where the evidence states none for that case the rule simply does not apply. It does not override requirement relevance — a bullet covering a `must_have` still outranks an unrelated bullet that happens to carry a number. And it is not a restatement of the rule above: that one governs two *angles on the same case*, where the vacancy's requirements decide which angle survives; this one governs two *different pieces of work* competing for the same slot. Never read this rule as "always choose the impact angle" — when both candidates describe the same case, the rule above decides, not this one.

=== SELECTED PROJECTS ===

Candidate personal projects available for selection (do not invent others unless the input context provides them):
- **AI Job Assistant** — a FastAPI/PostgreSQL personal project for job ingestion, deduplication and AI-assisted extraction workflows (tech_stack: Python, FastAPI, PostgreSQL, OpenAI API, GitHub Actions). `project_type: "personal_project"`.
- **Email Camp** — use only bullet content that is explicitly supported by the input context, otherwise mark its claims `needs evidence` rather than inventing detail. `project_type: "personal_project"`.

**Hard rule — never duplicate current-work content here.** JobFlow CV Pipeline belongs to `current_work_block` and must never also appear as a `selected_projects` entry: not with `include: true`, not under a reworded title, and not split into differently-phrased bullets. The same applies to anything else already described in `current_work_block` — if its substance is in that block, `selected_projects` must not describe it again, however relevant it is to this vacancy. When JobFlow is the only project that would fit the vacancy, return `selected_projects` as an empty array: an empty Selected Projects section is the established, correct outcome here, not a gap to fill. The `project_type: "current_work_project"` enum value exists only for the rare case where the input context explicitly describes a current-work project that is NOT part of `current_work_block`; it is never a licence to re-list JobFlow or any other current-work item.

**The duplication rule is symmetric.** It also runs the other way, and that direction is easier to miss because the two copies do not look alike. When a current-work bullet describes in generic terms — typically as continued learning or study — the same work a `selected_projects[]` entry describes concretely, with substantially the same stack, that is the same content in two places just as much as a re-listed JobFlow would be. Here the concrete project entry is the one that earns its space: it names what was built, covers requirements, and reads as work rather than as study. Resolve it on the current-work side, and **narrowing is the default**: rewrite that bullet to carry only what the project entry does not already cover, so the block keeps its bullet count. Dropping the bullet outright is allowed only when a genuinely different, evidence-backed current-work bullet is available to take the freed slot — the block must still hold 4-5 bullets afterwards (see CURRENT-WORK BLOCK above), and the inventory there is short, so in most cases no replacement exists and narrowing is the only correct resolution. A current-work block left at three bullets to resolve a duplication is a worse outcome than the duplication was. Never resolve it by cutting or thinning the project entry instead.

**Inclusion test — coverage, not topic.** Do not decide inclusion by whether a project belongs to an interesting category. Decide it by what the project adds to `requirement_coverage` that is not already there. Include a project (`include: true`) only when all of these hold:

1. It is not already covered by the current-work block (the hard rule above).
2. It covers at least one vacancy requirement — `must_have`, `nice_to_have` or `hidden` — that is currently `"not_shown"`, or that is shown only at `"transferable"` strength, and this project would raise it to `"partial"` or `"strong"`.
3. The commercial evidence already in the CV does not cover that requirement better. Commercial work outranks a personal project on the same requirement, always. A project that restates ground EPAM or Factor–IT already covers is displacing a stronger claim, not adding one.
4. Including it does not push the CV past its target page count at the cost of a requirement-covering commercial bullet.

Set `include: false` when the project fails any of the above, when it is unrelated to the vacancy's requirements, or when it creates overclaiming risk. `relevance_reason` must name the specific requirement the project covers and why commercial evidence does not cover it — not a general statement that the project is relevant. Use `display_priority` of `"must_show"`, `"show_if_space"` or `"hide_if_no_space"` to guide the renderer's overflow handling — never imply the renderer should invent relevance on its own. Never present these personal projects as commercial production work.

=== SAFETY / OVERCLAIMING CHECKS (apply before finalizing output) ===

Check and record findings in `overclaiming_check`:
- Personal AI/RAG/FastAPI/OpenAI exposure is never presented as commercial production experience.
- MCP / Claude Code — personal/portfolio tooling exposure only, not commercial production.
- Docker, NestJS, Kubernetes, MongoDB or AWS are never presented as commercial core skills without explicit supporting evidence in the input context.
- React/Next.js work stays framed as backend-focused fullstack contribution unless the vacancy clearly justifies frontend emphasis.
- A prior PHP role is never presented as a current target stack.
- Language levels stay honest: do not present German above the level stated in the input context (default A2/B1) or English as fluent (default B1/B1+ professional working use) unless the context states otherwise.
- Ownership claims are not inflated beyond what the evidence source supports (e.g. a team-maintained system is not claimed as sole ownership).
- Technical names match evidence verbatim, with no near-synonym substitution (see EVIDENCE SOURCE RULES).
- Every technology in every `tech_stack` belongs to the entry it is listed under, verified against that entry's own employer, period or project — not merely against the candidate's overall profile. Walk the entries one at a time and read each name against that entry's evidence: a correctly-spelled name sitting in the wrong entry passes the verbatim check above and is still a factual claim the candidate cannot defend (see CONTENT SELECTION).
- No internal audit reasoning has leaked into any `cv_content` field (see INTERNAL REASONING NEVER BECOMES PUBLIC CV TEXT).
- For early-stage/startup/product-engineer roles: direct customer collaboration, product discovery, product decisions, UX/copy ownership and founder-level decisions are listed in `needs_evidence` unless clearly supported by the context.
Anything that fails these checks goes into `critical_issues` (must be fixed/removed) or `warnings` (acceptable but flagged); unresolved unsupported claims go into `needs_evidence` and must also appear in `evidence_table` with status "needs evidence".

=== QUALITY SCORE RUBRIC (quality_score: 0-100) ===

`quality_score` is a self-assessment of how complete, well-evidenced and internally consistent THIS CV draft is — it is not a vacancy-fit score. Rate it by: whether every bullet cites a real `evidence_source` grounded in the provided context; whether `requirement_coverage` is complete (every `must_have` and `nice_to_have` accounted for) and internally consistent (every `shown_in` path really exists in the CV, no confirmed-evidence `must_have` left `"not_shown"` for a non-requirement bullet); whether `overclaiming_check` is clean (few/no `critical_issues`, few `needs_evidence` items); whether `evidence_table` entries are mostly "confirmed"/"cautious" rather than "needs evidence"/"remove"; whether the current-work block and mandatory structure are intact. Score 90-100 for a fully evidence-grounded draft with complete, consistent coverage and no unresolved overclaiming risk; 60-89 for a solid draft with a few flagged warnings, needs-evidence items or uncovered nice_to_have requirements; below 60 if the input context was too sparse to safely fill out the CV, several claims remain unresolved, or `must_have` requirements went uncovered despite available evidence.
