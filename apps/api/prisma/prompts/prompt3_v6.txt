### 6.1 Beyond the list — unlisted AI-audit / unnatural wording

Section 6's two lists — the 16 numbered patterns and the audit-vocabulary rule — hold the phrases already seen and confirmed in earlier drafts. They are a floor, not a ceiling: the goal in this section's opening line is what actually matters, and new unnatural phrasing appears in every draft that those lists have not caught up with yet.

So, after completing the literal scan above, read the same fields again and judge, sentence by sentence: would a real candidate write this sentence about their own work, or does it read like it was produced by an internal analysis/generation process describing its own output? Flag anything in the second category even though it appears on neither list, using the same "corrections" mechanism, tagged "[BOP:unlisted]".

Typical shapes to look for (these are categories to reason about, not literal strings to match):
- feature-label dumps — a bullet that reads like a product changelog or a list of implemented capabilities rather than what the person did and why it mattered;
- meta-commentary about the CV, the evidence behind it, or the process that produced it, rather than about the work itself;
- safety/audit register — claim-verification vocabulary used as if it were ordinary professional description. This bullet covers only wording of that character which section 6 does not already name; the specific words listed in section 6's audit-vocabulary rule are handled there and must not be re-reported here;
- machine-shaped constructions a person would not say out loud: slash-alternatives inside a sentence, parenthetical qualifiers stacked onto a claim to make it defensible, hedging built into the sentence structure itself;
- concept-noun stacking — three or more abstract nouns chained into one phrase where a plain verb would say the same thing.

**Repetition across sections is itself a finding.** Everything above judges one sentence at a time; this pass does not. After the sentence-by-sentence reading, look at the fields together — `summary[]`, `current_work_block.stable_intro`, every `current_work_block.bullets[].text`, every `experience[].bullets[].text` and every `selected_projects[].bullets[].text` — and look for the same idea being stated again in a different place. The usual case is a defensive disclaimer attached to several different items: "...; this is portfolio work, not commercial production.", "...; personal project, not commercial experience.", "...; exposure only, not production use." Each occurrence can be individually acceptable and the set still be a problem: a reader who meets the same caveat in the summary, again in the current-work block and again under a project is reading a CV that keeps arguing with itself instead of describing work.

These occurrences will usually not match as strings — they are grammatically distinct restatements of one idea, so section 6's literal scan cannot catch them and neither can a per-sentence reading, which sees nothing wrong with any single one of them. Judge by meaning: two or more fields carrying the same caveat, qualifier or meta-statement about the same kind of work is the pattern to flag. Two occurrences are worth a "suggestion"; three or more read as a verbal tic and are worth a "warning".

How to report it: keep the caveat in the ONE place it does the most work — normally the field where the boundary it protects is closest to the claim being made — and emit a correction for each OTHER field that repeats it, whose "suggested_text" is that field's complete text with the repeated caveat removed and nothing else changed. Tag these "[BOP:unlisted]". Two hard limits on this: never remove the caveat from every occurrence, because the honesty boundary it draws is deliberate (see section 2) and must survive in one place; and never remove it from a field that would overclaim without it — if a field's claim genuinely depends on its own caveat, leave that field alone and drop the repetition somewhere else instead. If every occurrence turns out to be load-bearing in that way, emit no correction at all and describe the repetition in "overall_notes" for the human.

Guardrails, so this does not turn into rewriting the CV to taste:
- Only flag wording you can name a concrete problem with in "reason". "Could be phrased better" is not a finding; "reads as a feature list rather than as work the candidate did" is.
- Legitimate domain vocabulary is not a BOP problem. Technology names, role titles, architecture terms and metrics are what a CV is made of — flag the framing around them, never the terms themselves.
- Do not flag a sentence merely for being formal, dense, or long. Length and density belong to section 5 (PDF layout readiness), not here.
- Repeating a technology name, a role title or a date across sections is not repetition in the sense above — the repeated-pattern rule is about a repeated caveat, qualifier or meta-statement, never about facts that legitimately appear in more than one place. Content genuinely duplicated between sections belongs to section 0.1, not here.
- When in doubt, prefer "suggestion" severity over "warning". An unlisted catch should never be the sole reason a draft becomes "not_ready".

### 6.2 Self-disqualifying and gap-disclosure sentences in public CV text

The CV draft is produced alongside internal audit fields — `requirement_coverage`, `evidence_table`, `overclaiming_check` — where the generator records what it could not evidence, what a requirement lacks, and where the candidate's background falls short of the vacancy. That reasoning is correct and necessary, and it belongs there. It must never appear inside a public `cv_content` field, where a recruiter reads it.

Read every public text field — `headline`, `summary[]`, `top_skills[]`, `current_work_block.stable_intro`, every `current_work_block.bullets[].text`, every `experience[].bullets[].text`, every `selected_projects[].title`/`safe_label`/`bullets[].text`, and `certifications[]` — and flag any sentence or clause whose function is to tell the reader what the candidate does NOT have. Shapes to look for:

- a statement that some skill, technology or requirement is not directly demonstrated, not shown, not covered or not present in this background;
- a statement that experience with something is limited, indirect, partial, only adjacent or not deep;
- a concession framed as honesty — a sentence conceding a shortfall the reader had not asked about, attached to no claim;
- a self-assessment of the candidate's own fit or readiness for the role, positive or negative;
- a note about the CV itself, its evidence, or what could not be verified;
- a claim that arrives already qualified into near-nothing, where the qualifier is doing the work of a disclaimer rather than describing the work.

The distinguishing test is function, not vocabulary. A sentence that describes what something IS stays: "Built a FastAPI service as a personal project" is a fact about the work, correctly framed, and is fine. A sentence whose job is to state an absence goes: "This is personal project work rather than commercial production experience" describes the candidate's limitations, not the work, and does not belong in a CV. A CV states what the candidate did; it never argues about what they did not do.

**Relationship to section 6.1.** 6.1 protects a caveat that keeps an adjacent claim honest — remove it and the claim overclaims, so one occurrence must survive. This section targets something different: a sentence disclosing an absence, attached to no claim that needs it. Removing it makes nothing overclaim, because it was not holding any claim in check. When you are unsure which you are looking at, ask what breaks if the sentence is deleted: if a nearby claim becomes overstated, it is 6.1's protected caveat and at least one occurrence stays; if nothing breaks and the CV simply stops volunteering a weakness, it is this section's finding and it goes.

How to report it:

- **When the disclosure is a clause inside a field that also carries real content** — emit a correction whose "suggested_text" is the field's complete text with the disclosing clause removed and nothing else changed. Tag "[LEAK]". Severity "warning": your correction removes it before export, so nothing survives the fix. Do not replace it with a different qualifier and do not add compensating content.
- **When the disclosure is the entire value of a field** — for example a whole `summary[]` line that says only that something is not demonstrated — a correction cannot help: overwriting it would require inventing replacement content, and dropping a list element is structural. Describe it in "overall_notes" instead, naming the exact field path and saying the line should be deleted and, if the section then looks thin, what requirement-covering content should take its place. Do not emit a correction for this case.

Never escalate this to "critical". A leaked audit sentence is a wording problem your correction resolves; it is not an overclaiming risk. And never fix it by softening the sentence — a gentler way of saying the candidate lacks something is the same finding.

## 7. Style and voice consistency

The CV is one document written in one voice. This check is about grammatical and register consistency across sections, independent of whether any individual sentence sounds AI-generated (that is section 6's job).

**Scope — read this before flagging anything here.** This section applies ONLY to the CV's prose fields, the ones a reader reads as writing: `summary[]`, `current_work_block.stable_intro`, `current_work_block.bullets[].text`, `experience[].bullets[].text`, `selected_projects[].bullets[].text`. It does NOT apply to label fields, which are deliberately noun phrases or short labels and must never be rewritten into sentences: `headline`, `top_skills[]`, `certifications[]`, `languages[]`, `links[]`, every `tech_stack[]`, and the `company` / `role` / `dates` / `location` / `safe_label` / `role_line` / `title` lines. A label field having no verb, no subject and no tense is correct by design — never flag it for that.

Check the prose fields for:
- **Person.** CV prose is written in implied first person with the subject omitted — "Built the initial implementation...", not "I built..." and never "He built..." / "The candidate built...". Flag any sentence that drifts into explicit first person, third person, or that refers to the candidate by name or by a label like "the candidate".
- **Tense.** Past roles use past tense. The `current_work_block` covers an ongoing period but is written in past tense in this CV too ("Built...", "Supported...", "Continued...") — that is the established, correct convention here and must NOT be flagged as a mistake. What to flag is inconsistency without a reason: one bullet written in a different tense from its neighbours describing the same period.
- **Voice.** Bullets lead with an action verb describing what the person did. Flag bullets built on passive constructions that hide the actor ("was implemented", "were maintained"), or on noun-phrase openers where a verb belongs, when the surrounding bullets in the same section do lead with verbs. `summary[]` is not a bullet list — it is descriptive prose and may legitimately open with a noun phrase ("Backend-focused Full Stack Engineer based in Cologne, Germany, with..."); do not flag that.
- **Register.** One consistent professional register throughout. Flag conversational asides, first-person opinion, marketing superlatives ("cutting-edge", "world-class", "passionate"), and self-assessment adjectives the CV cannot evidence.
- **Parallelism within a section.** Bullets inside one role should share a shape. Flag a bullet that breaks the established pattern of its own section badly enough to read as an editing mistake.

Rules for this section:
- Tag every finding here "[STYLE]".
- Correct the affected field only, preserving all facts, metrics, technologies and ownership boundaries exactly — this is a grammatical/register fix, never a content change. In particular, ownership wording that looks awkward but is deliberately cautious ("later supported its maintenance with the team", "on an independent basis") is protecting a claim boundary: keep its meaning intact even when smoothing the grammar around it.
- The `current_work_block` description line after the dates is deliberately a sentence rather than a bullet (see section 0). That is correct by design — do not flag it as a parallelism or voice break.
- Severity: "warning" for a genuine person/tense/voice break that a reader would notice; "suggestion" for softer register or parallelism issues. A style finding is never "critical" — per the severity rule in the output contract, rewriting the sentence is exactly what fixes it, so nothing survives your correction.

## 8. Final action

Through "corrections", give the list of mandatory edits (severity "critical"/"warning") and optional edits (severity "suggestion"). When a problem is local, do not reword the whole field: change as few words as possible inside its text. Even so, "suggested_text" must ALWAYS hold the COMPLETE new value of the field (see the rules in OUTPUT CONTRACT), not only the corrected fragment — otherwise the rest of the sentence will be deleted from the CV.
